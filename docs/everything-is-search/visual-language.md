# Visual language — draft grammar

The central design challenge is to show **possibilities, accessibility, activity, and persistence as different things**. The previous Concept Space review demonstrated how easily one visual cue can accidentally mean several of them.

## Define the space before animating it

Every example needs a short answer to four questions:

- What does a location represent?
- What does moving or changing location represent?
- What does the boundary mean?
- What does a trace remaining on screen mean?

These answers can differ by example, but their changes must be introduced rather than silently inherited.

## Two boundaries: a question to settle

The creator proposes an outer sphere for all concepts that could ever be reached by the search, with an internal region showing limits on reaching that whole.

There are two possible readings:

| Reading | Outer boundary | Inner boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: current conditions versus wider conditions | Possibilities available across the broader conditions admitted by this example | Reachable under current constraints, tools, starting point, and allowed changes |
| B: absolute rules versus available resources | Everything permitted by fixed rules | What is practically accessible within a chosen time, resource, or path constraint |

**Proposal:** prototype reading A first. It makes it possible to change an inner constraint without claiming that something escaped “all that is possible.” This is not yet a decision.

Do not call the outer region “everything this exact search could ever reach under these unchanged rules” and then put a permanently unreachable region inside it. The label must tell us which conditions are held fixed.

A boundary can be unknown or uncertain. A schematic outer circle or sphere is a framing device, not evidence that the real space is finite, completely known, or naturally spherical.

## Candidate layers

| Layer | Question it answers | Proposed representation | Avoid confusing it with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Contextual possibility | What possibilities are admitted in this example? | Quiet outer frame | A mapped, measurable universe |
| Present reach | What is accessible under the current conditions? | Irregular outline or translucent region, possibly disconnected | Territory already visited |
| Current activity | Where is change happening now? | Moving markers, local pulses, multiple walkers where appropriate | The entire reachable set |
| History | Where has this process been? | Trails or a separately toggled accumulated layer | Current attention or retained capability |
| Persistence | What remains, and in what sense? | An example-specific cue introduced explicitly | Universal fitness, importance, or correctness |
| Constraints | What restricts available changes? | Gates, barriers, allowed links, or boundary changes | All restrictions being literal walls |

Not all layers should appear together. Introduce one distinction at a time and show only what the current question needs.

## Start in 2D, then introduce 3D

Confirmed direction: begin in 2D and build to 3D. A line may precede this only if it helps.

A possible opening:

1. Place a mark on a simple plane: one possibility.
2. Add alternatives: a space of possibilities rather than a single thing.
3. Introduce a rule allowing some changes and excluding others.
4. Let a process leave a visible trail.
5. Separate that trail from the larger region it could have explored.
6. Add a third independent choice and reveal depth.
7. Explain that this is still a deliberately small picture of a potentially much richer space.

Avoid saying three dimensions are all humans can visualize. We can depict additional variables through color, time, facets, labels, and multiple views. The useful claim is that a navigable 3D scene is a chosen spatial metaphor, with limitations. Projecting many distinctions into three coordinates can distort distance, apparent overlap, and neighborhood.

**Proposal:** demonstrate one distortion instead of only disclaiming it. Two points that look adjacent in one view can be separated by a hidden distinction. Introduce this gently; do not let the explanation derail the opening.

## What “growth” might mean

The creator wants search to animate as it grows inside the space. Before implementing a scene, identify which growth is intended:

- More territory has been visited, while reach is unchanged.
- More activity is happening within already visited territory.
- More possibilities become accessible because a condition changes.
- Something accumulates or persists after exploration.
- The space's description gains a new variable or new kinds of possibility.

A growing illuminated cloud must not stand for all five. Likewise, do not make growth inevitable: a process may revisit, narrow, branch, stagnate, lose access, or forget, if that is the phenomenon being illustrated.

## Candidate interactions

- Change one constraint while holding the initial state and other rules fixed.
- Compare a short run with a longer run without changing the reachable boundary.
- Introduce a new allowed move and inspect what becomes accessible.
- Replay the same conditions with a different random sequence, if the example uses randomness.
- Compare no trace, fading trace, and persistent trace as explicitly different toy rules.
- Show several simultaneous paths rather than implying a single explorer.
- Let a prior result change a later condition, once the viewer understands the simpler case.

These are proposed teaching interactions. A simulation's behavior is evidence about its specified rules, not automatic evidence about the real-world example it resembles.

## Lessons to carry forward from Concept Space

- Preserve both points and surfaces, but make their meanings explicit.
- Use stable shared-membership colors when the same relation recurs.
- Expose interior overlap rather than hiding it behind an opaque exterior.
- Use distinct markers for attention; do not make dimness also mean loss of reach.
- Keep before/after views aligned when the lesson depends on a subtle change.
- Anchor notes to the actual feature and protect the central scene from unnecessary text.
- Retain the expansive scene and restrained cinematic narrative.
- Treat a line as a semantic claim: connection, transition, communication, and divergence should not accidentally look identical.

## Open visual experiments

- A small 2D toy world with separate reachable and visited layers.
- Two runs with identical reach but very different histories.
- A constraint opening a new route without changing the outer frame.
- A process with no destination marker and several different persistence rules.
- A controlled 2D-to-3D reveal that teaches an additional degree of freedom.

These should be inexpensive studies before a complete site redesign.

## Local possibilities and the zoom outward — endorsed direction

The creator wants tangible, fine-grained examples before the broader view. They connect the changing availability of next steps to “the adjacent possible.”

Distinguish three scopes when useful:

- **Available next changes:** transitions permitted from the current situation under the example's rules.
- **Reachable through a sequence:** possibilities accessible through multiple changes. Some may require intermediates that are not yet present.
- **The broader frame:** the wider collection of possibilities admitted by the example, whose precise boundary remains unresolved.

Do not equate these with three fixed concentric shells. An available transition may be a link rather than a short Euclidean distance. Screen proximity should not silently define what is possible next.

### Proposed visual sequence

1. Show a concrete, legible state or object, with enough detail to understand what can change.
2. Reveal a small selection of possible next changes. Label this a selection if it is not exhaustive.
3. Follow one change. Keep unchosen alternatives distinguishable from the path actually taken.
4. Reveal how the available next changes differ from those before. Do not imply every step necessarily expands the available set.
5. Pull back, keeping the chosen object or event identifiable, to expose a longer sequence.
6. Pull back farther to show other paths or interacting processes where the example warrants them.
7. Allow a return to the original local event so the abstract overview stays connected to its concrete meaning.

This is a proposed grammar, not a universal branching-tree model. Some examples may require merging paths, loops, simultaneous changes, or interactions that alter the available transitions.

### Continuity across scale

The zoom should explain the relationship between scales, not merely crossfade from an object to an unrelated cloud. Carry a recognizable marker, color, label, or highlighted path through the transition. State when the unit of representation changes—for example, from one event to an aggregate of many events.

At close range, show an object, action, or state the viewer can describe. At wider scales, progressively simplify marks while retaining the link back to that example. The first close-up need not itself be a dot floating in an abstract sphere.
